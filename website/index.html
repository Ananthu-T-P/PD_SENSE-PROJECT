01

The substantia nigra, when it works

Deep in the midbrain sits a thin, darkly pigmented band of neurons — the substantia nigra, "black substance," named for the neuromelanin in its cells. These neurons produce dopamine and deliver it to the basal ganglia, the cluster of structures that lets the brain select and start movements.

Every time you reach for a cup, take a first step, or swing your arms as you walk, that dopamine signal is part of what lets the movement begin smoothly — automatically, without conscious effort.

02

What degrades

In Parkinson's disease, the dopamine-producing neurons of the substantia nigra progressively die. As dopamine delivery to the basal ganglia falls, the brain's ability to initiate and regulate movement erodes with it. This is not damage spread across the brain — it is concentrated loss in one small, specific structure.

By the time motor symptoms become noticeable, a substantial share of these neurons is already gone — which is why the disease can be underway for years before anyone sees it.

03

What that causes in the body

Resting tremor — a rhythmic shaking, often starting in one hand, that appears when the limb is at rest and eases with deliberate movement.

Bradykinesia — slowness of movement. Buttoning a shirt or typing takes visibly longer; fine motor tasks lose their automatic ease.

Rigidity — muscle stiffness that resists movement and can ache.

Postural instability — impaired balance and a tendency to fall.

Freezing of gait — the feet briefly refuse to move, as if glued to the floor, most often when starting to walk, turning, or passing through a doorway.

These are measurable, physical signals — which is exactly why a wearable sensor can track them.

Prototype status

PD-SENSE is an engineering prototype. Live telemetry never appears on this public site: device data flows from the wristband to the PD-SENSE backend and is visible only in the authenticated Live Monitor and Doctor Portal.

Open the live monitor → Doctor portal →

One wristband. Built to measure all of it.

PD-SENSE is a wrist-worn monitor built on an ESP32 with stacked sensor boards. It runs short guided tests — tremor, tap, gait — scores the result on the spot with sound and haptic feedback, and streams a timestamped history to the doctor-facing portal.

  • On-device scoring. FFT tremor detection runs on the ESP32 itself. No phone, no app, no cloud dependency in the room.
  • Immediate feedback. Buzzer and haptic cues tell the patient when a test starts, ends, or flags an event.
  • Clinician timeline. The portal accumulates snapshots into trends, best/worst-day comparisons, and medication cross-references.
Request clinical access

PD-SENSE measures and records motor symptoms. It does not diagnose, and it does not replace clinical judgment. Every trend it reports is a summary of measured movement for a clinician to interpret.
